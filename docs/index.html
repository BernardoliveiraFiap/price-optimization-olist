The problem

Price is not assigned at random

Two traps sit between a dataset and a price list. The first is statistical, the second is about decisions. Most analyses fall into both.

One number decides everything on this page

, written β, answers a single question: if I raise the price by 1%, how much do sales fall? A β of −1.4 means sales fall 1.4%, and it runs both ways: cut the price 1% and sales rise 1.4%. It is a rate, not a quantity — like kilometres per litre, on its own it predicts nothing until you say how far the price moved. It comes out negative because price and quantity move in opposite directions, so a positive β would be claiming people buy more when you charge more. The line that matters is −1: past it (more negative) a price rise loses more volume than it gains in price and revenue falls, while short of it a price rise still grows revenue. Every estimate on this page is one more attempt at that same number.

Trap 1: the confound

Suppose you record the price and the occupancy of a hundred restaurants. The expensive ones are fuller. Does raising prices fill a restaurant?

Of course not. The expensive restaurant is expensive because it is good. You did not measure the effect of price; you measured the effect of quality, which was hiding behind price. Statistics has a name for that hidden third thing: a . The same thing happens to products: a seller charges more for exactly the items people want most.

The direction of the error is always the same

Demand reacts to price far more than this calculation shows. In business terms: raising prices looks safe when it is not. On the data, a plain regression returns β = −0.002. Read it out loud and it says a 1% price rise would cost 0.002% of sales, which is to say nothing at all. No market behaves that way. The effect was not shrunk, it was erased.

Trap 2: an elasticity is not a price

Suppose the elasticity is correct. There is a textbook formula that turns it into a price, one line long, and this project keeps it in two files (synth.py and impact.py). It is the profit-maximising price of a single product whose demand curve never bends:

best price = cost × β ÷ (1 + β)
β = −1.44Watches and gifts, a category that passes the gate. The formula says charge 3.26 times the unit cost, and that is a usable answer.
β = −0.756The whole portfolio. The same formula returns a negative price: with demand this calm there is no best price, only “charge more, forever”. It is the same reason the gate demands β past −1.05.

So the formula breaks on most of the catalogue, needs a unit cost Olist never recorded, and prices one product as though the other 74 did not exist. Cut one price and another has to carry the average back up. That is not arithmetic any more, and it is why a solver sits at the end of the chain.

The four guardrails, and how to read each number

The portfolio is a concrete list: the 75 products in the three categories that cleared the gate, 23% of the 's revenue, priced in one shot. Four rules bound what may happen to that list, and all four are constants in config.py rather than numbers invented for a slide. A ceiling is the most you are allowed; a floor is the least you must keep; a band is how far a single price may travel in either direction.

The approach

Estimate, validate, decide, price the error

Five stages. Each card says what goes in, what comes out, and the number that actually came out. The fifth is the one that decided this project: the pipeline is allowed to say no.

1

Estimate

In: 16,278 rows, one per product and week. Out: three answers to one question: −0.002, −0.756, +58.4.

2

Validate

In: an invented market, true elasticity −2.103 written down first. Out: how far each estimator missed it.

3

Decide

In: 75 products, 11 candidate prices each. Out: one price per product, all four guardrails obeyed.

4

Price the error

In: the biased estimate and the credible one. Out: the margin lost by trusting the wrong one, and the range behind the headline.

5

Refuse

Two gates. An under 10 kills the instrument; a weak or uncertain category gets no price. Both fire: 77% of revenue gets no recommendation.

Architecture

How the pieces connect

Read it left to right, in the numbered order. Two entry points feed the same chain: a synthetic market where the right answer is known in advance, and the real Olist CSV files. Every box is a module that runs and is tested on its own, and the line under each box says what it does.

Box 3, the gate, is where this project earns its keep

Every other box computes something. This one decides whether the pipeline is allowed to answer at all, and it asks two questions in order. First: is the instrument strong enough to be used, meaning is its at least 10? Here it is 0.16, so 2SLS is thrown away and fixed effects carry the estimate. Second, per category: is this elasticity past −1.05 and at least twice its own ? Three categories out of twenty say yes. The other seventeen leave with no price, which is why the box is drawn as a wall rather than a step.

Results

What the numbers say

Four questions, six charts. Under each one, whether the number is good news or bad news, and for whom.

Can the estimators find an answer that is already known?

This chart needs one sentence of setup, because the rest of the page leans on it. The code builds a market that does not exist, and it builds it by choosing the elasticity: −2.103, written into the generator before any model sees a single row. That number is the answer key. Running the three estimators over that invented market shows how far each one lands from an answer known with certainty, which is exactly the check nobody can run on real data. That is what the invented market is for.

On the real data, which categories can be priced at all?

What do the guardrails cost, and how much of the answer is an assumption?

The best forecaster is the worst pricing model

A fair objection to all of this is: skip the econometrics, throw a strong model at the data. So that was done. A was trained to predict next week's units, judged only on weeks it had never seen. The left chart is the forecasting job, the right chart asks that same trained model what it thinks would happen if the price moved. It wins the first job and loses the second, and it loses in the dangerous direction.
