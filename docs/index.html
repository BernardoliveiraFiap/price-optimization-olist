The problem

Price is not assigned at random

Two traps sit between a dataset and a price list. The first is statistical, the second is about decisions. Most analyses fall into both.

Trap 1: the confound

Suppose you record the price and the occupancy of a hundred restaurants. The expensive ones are fuller. Does raising prices fill a restaurant?

Of course not. The expensive restaurant is expensive because it is good. You did not measure the effect of price; you measured the effect of quality, which was hiding behind price. The same thing happens to products: a seller charges more for exactly the items people want most.

The direction of the error is always the same

Demand looks less price-sensitive than it is. In business terms: raising prices looks safe when it is not. On the Olist data, naive OLS returns −0.002. The effect is not shrunk, it is erased.

Trap 2: an elasticity is not a price

Suppose the elasticity is correct. The optimal price for one product in isolation is a textbook formula, and it is useless, because no pricing team moves one product alone. Real constraints couple the whole portfolio:

GuardrailWhat it forbids
Average priceRaising the portfolio's mean price beyond a cap
RevenueLetting total revenue fall
VolumeLosing units sold
Per-product bandMoving any single price more than ±30%

Drop one price and another has to carry the average. That is no longer arithmetic; it is a constrained optimization problem, which is why a MILP sits at the end of the chain.

The approach

Estimate, validate, decide, price the error

Four steps, plus a fifth property that turned out to matter more than any of them: the pipeline is allowed to say no.

1

Estimate

Three estimators, each assuming less about where prices came from. Three numbers reveal the size of the bias; one number reveals nothing.

2

Validate

A synthetic market is generated whose true elasticity is written before anything is fitted. On real data the answer is unobservable, so “my model says −1.4” cannot be checked by anyone. Here it can.

3

Decide

A mixed-integer program picks one price per product from a discrete grid, subject to every portfolio guardrail at once. The output is a price list a team could execute, not a coefficient.

4

Price the error

How much margin is lost by acting on the biased estimate, and how fast the conclusion decays if the demand curve is wrong. This is the step that connects statistics to money.

5

Refuse

Two gates stand between an estimate and a price list, and on the real data both fire. An instrument with a first-stage F below 10 is not used; a category that is not credibly elastic is not priced. 77% of panel revenue receives no recommendation. That is the correct answer, not a gap to fill by lowering the bar.

The finding worth the conversation

The variant with demand history is the less biased of the two, which looks backwards until you notice what lagged demand is: a product's own recent sales encode its latent quality, so the lags absorb unit-level heterogeneity the way a fixed effect does. Badly, but not by nothing. The features that make the model a good forecaster are the same ones that partially clean its elasticity. Neither effect was designed.

What this does not establish
